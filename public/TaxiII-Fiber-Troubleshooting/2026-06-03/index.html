The short version

  • Good  Our Wi-Fi signal and our own devices are not the problem — the wireless link is excellent (strong signal, full-speed connection).
  • Problem  The connection is losing a large share of its data packets — roughly 20% to 56% in repeated tests. That alone is enough to drag a 1 Gbps line down to 50–100 Mbps, because the loss forces the connection to keep slowing itself down.
  • Context  The loss begins at the very first device our traffic reaches and grows as it travels upstream. This device is building-managed equipment, and our unit was reportedly connected by “splitting an existing pipe” — a detail worth investigating.
  • New  This is recent. The same laptop tested clean (0% loss) just a few days ago, on May 30, 2026.

What we found — in two levels of detail

Plain English

Our Wi-Fi and devices are healthy

We checked the wireless connection between our laptop and the box that provides our internet. The signal is very strong — about as good as it gets — and the laptop is connecting at full speed.

So the trouble is not our gadgets, not the distance to the router, and not old equipment. Something further along the line is the issue.

Technical

Wi-Fi air link is not the bottleneck

Client associated at 802.11ax, 5 GHz ch 149 (80 MHz), RSSI −41 dBm, noise −94 dBm (SNR ≈ 53 dB), PHY rate 1200 Mbps, MCS 11, WPA3. These are near-ideal RF conditions.

Crucially, netstat -I on the Wi-Fi interface showed zero input/output errors despite heavy loss — ruling out a driver/NIC/airtime hardware fault on the client side. Strong signal + high loss points upstream, not at the radio.

Plain English

A lot of data is getting lost

Think of the connection as a delivery route. When we sent test packages along it, somewhere between 1-in-5 and more than half never arrived.

When that happens, the internet automatically slows everything down to cope. That is exactly why a 1-gigabit line ends up feeling like 50–100 Mbps: the speed is fine, but the losses keep forcing it to throttle back.

Technical

20–56% packet loss is collapsing TCP throughput

Repeated ICMP tests showed loss ranging 20% to 56% to public anchors (1.1.1.1 and 8.8.8.8), with low, stable RTT (3–13 ms) on surviving packets — i.e. loss-dominated, not buffer/latency-dominated. A brief 1003 ms spike appeared early, then disappeared, consistent with intermittent events.

At these loss rates, TCP congestion control (and loss-based AIMD in particular) cannot sustain a large congestion window, so goodput collapses far below line rate regardless of provisioned speed. This fully accounts for the observed 50–100 Mbps floors and 500–600 Mbps recoveries.

Plain English

The losses start right at the first box

We traced the route step by step. The losses are already happening at the very first piece of equipment our connection touches — and they get worse as the data travels onward toward the wider internet.

That points the finger at the equipment and wiring closest to us, rather than at the far-off internet.

Technical

Loss originates at hop 1 and compounds upstream

Per-hop loss in Sample B: gateway 32% → first WAN hop 40% → destination 56%. This monotonic increase indicates multiple loss contributors: the first local segment plus additional upstream loss across the access/aggregation path.

Honest caveat: ICMP to an intermediate hop can be inflated by control-plane policing, so per-hop figures are indicative, not definitive. The authoritative signal is the end-to-end loss to the public anchors (20–56%), which is real on-path loss. The traceroute also shows no second RFC1918 hop — hop 2 is already a public Lumen/CenturyLink address — suggesting no additional NAT layer in the L3 path.

Plain English

The first box is building-managed equipment

The box that gives us internet is not a standard home router — it is equipment managed by the building's network provider. We were told our apartment was connected by “splitting an existing pipe” because they had run out of separate lines.

That kind of shared or split connection is worth a close look, because it can introduce exactly the sort of losses we are seeing. We cannot inspect this box ourselves — it requires a login the building/provider controls.

Technical

Gateway = managed MDU CPE; shared/split feed suspected

Gateway 192.168.0.1 fingerprint:

  • MAC 00:58:28:6c:89:83 → OUI Axon Networks Inc. (managed Wi-Fi / MDU vendor).
  • TLS cert O=GWS SW, CN=CPE (Irvine, CA), self-signed, issued 2026-04-08; server lighttpd; admin UI is login-gated.

Because the resident has “split an existing pipe” for service, plausible upstream causes include an added optical splitter reducing the ONT optical budget (low Rx power → FEC/CRC errors) or a shared/over-subscribed physical run. The decisive data — optical Rx/Tx power (dBm), FEC/CRC/uncorrectable counters, and PON utilization — sits behind the managed-CPE login and must be pulled by Quantum and/or the property's managed-network operator (Axon).

Plain English

It is new, and it comes and goes

This started only this week, with no obvious time-of-day pattern. The very same laptop tested perfectly clean just a few days ago. And it affects all three of our devices, which all connect wirelessly through that same building box.

Technical

Regression vs. a known-clean baseline

Baseline on the same client, May 30, 2026: ~280 Mbps download, 0% loss, low latency, no bufferbloat under load. Today's heavy loss is therefore a recent regression, not a longstanding condition. Loss rates are non-stationary (23% → 56% within ~20 min), and all household clients share the same managed AP, so a shared upstream impairment explains the fleet-wide, intermittent symptom.

What we would respectfully ask Quantum to check

We know the equipment may be partly building-managed, so some of this may need coordination with the property's network provider. We are happy to help however we can — including running any further tests you suggest. With thanks:

  1. Confirm our line is provisioned for 1 Gbps and that the account/ONT shows the expected service profile.
  2. Read the optical and error statistics at the ONT/CPE for our unit — particularly optical receive power (dBm) and FEC/CRC / uncorrectable-error counters. Low optical power or rising error counts would confirm a degraded physical feed.
  3. Investigate the recently “split” feed serving our apartment — whether a splitter, shared run, or marginal splice was introduced when our unit was connected, and whether it is within optical-budget spec.
  4. Check for sharing / over-subscription on that segment that could cause intermittent loss when neighboring usage is high.
  5. Coordinate with the property's managed-network provider (Axon Networks) if the gateway is managed, so the AP and its backhaul can be examined as well.
